# Hall of Fame

The Hall of Fame exists to recognise the projects, tools and communities that made modern retro preservation possible.

This is not a trophy cabinet for whatever happens to be popular this month. A Hall of Fame entry should have lasting importance. It should explain what the project contributed, why people still depend on it and what lesson future preservation work can learn from it.

The entries in this chapter will grow over time. The first version establishes the standard and names the obvious pillars.

## Selection Criteria

A project belongs in the Hall of Fame when it has made a clear contribution to one or more of these areas:

- preservation accuracy;
- documentation;
- emulation quality;
- accessibility;
- metadata and library presentation;
- historical understanding;
- long-term community value.

Popularity alone is not enough. A project can be widely used and still be badly documented, legally careless or technically fragile. The Hall of Fame is about durable contribution.

## MAME

**Category:** Emulator and preservation project.

**Why it matters:** MAME is one of the most important preservation projects in computing history. It documents arcade hardware and provides emulation for thousands of arcade systems, many of which would otherwise be difficult to study or experience.

**Historical context:** Arcade machines were not simple game files. They were hardware platforms, control panels, boards, sound chips, copy protection systems and display behaviours. Preserving them requires more than launching a ROM. It requires documenting how the machine worked.

**Legacy:** MAME taught the wider emulation world that preservation can be documentation as much as playback. Its influence reaches far beyond arcades.

**DAP note:** MAME should be treated as its own ecosystem. Arcade sets, ROM versions and emulator versions are closely linked. Random arcade ZIP files are a recipe for pain.

## Redump

**Category:** Disc preservation project.

**Why it matters:** Redump focuses on accurate optical disc dumping and verification. It helps identify known-good disc images and documents regional and revision differences.

**Historical context:** Optical media created a preservation problem that cartridges did not: tracks, offsets, multi-track layouts, copy protection and drive behaviour all matter. A disc image is not automatically correct because it boots.

**Legacy:** Redump gives disc-based preservation a shared reference point. For systems such as PlayStation, PlayStation 2, GameCube, Wii, Dreamcast and many computer formats, that shared reference is essential.

**DAP note:** Redump is about verification and documentation. It is not a frontend and not a download recommendation.

## No-Intro

**Category:** ROM preservation and DAT project.

**Why it matters:** No-Intro provides clean naming and verification data for cartridge and digital content preservation. It is widely used to identify known dumps and organise collections.

**Historical context:** Early ROM collections were often messy, with intros, trainers, bad dumps and inconsistent naming. No-Intro helped move preservation toward cleaner, verified sets.

**Legacy:** No-Intro remains one of the core references for cartridge-based and digital preservation workflows.

**DAP note:** A No-Intro style workflow is ideal when you want a clean library that can be audited rather than a folder full of mystery files.

## TOSEC

**Category:** Preservation catalogue.

**Why it matters:** TOSEC is especially important for computer systems, where software libraries often include tapes, disks, applications, demos, cover disks and obscure regional releases.

**Historical context:** Home computers such as the Amiga, Atari ST, Commodore 64, ZX Spectrum, Amstrad CPC and many others have messy software histories. A console-style approach is not always enough.

**Legacy:** TOSEC helps document the chaotic richness of computer software libraries.

**DAP note:** TOSEC can be especially useful for the machines that defined British and European home computing culture.

## ScummVM

**Category:** Game engine reimplementation.

**Why it matters:** ScummVM preserves access to adventure games and other supported titles by reimplementing game engines rather than emulating a whole machine.

**Historical context:** Many classic adventure games were tied to specific interpreters, operating systems and audio setups. ScummVM made them easier to run on modern systems while preserving behaviour and usability.

**Legacy:** It shows that preservation is not always full-system emulation. Sometimes the best route is understanding and recreating the engine.

**DAP note:** ScummVM libraries need careful folder structure. A random pile of archives will make frontend integration harder than it needs to be.

## Dolphin

**Category:** Emulator.

**Why it matters:** Dolphin is a landmark emulator for GameCube and Wii. It combines high compatibility, strong Linux support, active development and quality-of-life features such as enhancements, controller profiles and modern display options.

**Historical context:** GameCube and Wii preservation involves more than raw performance. Disc formats, motion controls, memory cards, save data, NAND behaviour and controller mapping all matter.

**Legacy:** Dolphin became one of the clearest examples of an emulator that is both technically serious and usable by ordinary players.

**DAP note:** RVZ is often the preferred archival format for Dolphin workflows because it balances compression, verification features and emulator support.

## PCSX2

**Category:** Emulator.

**Why it matters:** PCSX2 made PlayStation 2 emulation practical for a huge library of games. The PS2 catalogue is historically enormous, culturally important and technically varied.

**Historical context:** The PlayStation 2 was complex hardware. Getting games to run accurately required years of work and many improvements in CPU, graphics, audio and timing emulation.

**Legacy:** PCSX2 is central to preserving and accessing one of the most important console libraries ever released.

**DAP note:** BIOS organisation matters. PS2 emulation should be documented with clear region, path and configuration notes rather than guesswork.

## RPCS3

**Category:** Emulator.

**Why it matters:** RPCS3 is one of the most impressive modern emulation projects. PlayStation 3 hardware was notoriously unusual, and preserving access to that library requires serious engineering.

**Historical context:** The PS3 combined Blu-ray media, firmware, patches, digital content and a complex processor architecture. A working PS3 preservation setup must consider more than the base disc.

**Legacy:** RPCS3 proves that difficult modern systems can be documented and emulated through long-term open development.

**DAP note:** PS3 libraries need careful handling of disc games, digital packages, updates, DLC and licence files. This is not a drag-and-drop system.

## RetroArch and libretro

**Category:** Emulator framework and frontend layer.

**Why it matters:** RetroArch and libretro provide a shared framework for many emulator cores, shaders, controller profiles, netplay features and platform ports.

**Historical context:** Before common frameworks, every emulator tended to have its own interface, input system and configuration style. libretro created a more unified layer, though not without complexity.

**Legacy:** RetroArch made many systems more accessible across devices and platforms.

**DAP note:** RetroArch is powerful, but it is not always the simplest option. Standalone emulators may be better for some systems.

## ScreenScraper

**Category:** Metadata and media database.

**Why it matters:** ScreenScraper helps frontends display games with artwork, descriptions, screenshots, videos and structured metadata.

**Historical context:** As retro libraries moved from folders to visual frontends, metadata became important for usability. A clean library is easier to browse when the presentation layer is rich.

**Legacy:** ScreenScraper is part of the modern frontend ecosystem and is especially useful for ES-DE style setups.

**DAP note:** Metadata improves presentation. It does not verify ROMs or replace preservation databases.

## SteamGridDB

**Category:** Artwork and library presentation.

**Why it matters:** SteamGridDB provides artwork that helps non-Steam games look at home inside Steam and Steam-like launchers.

**Historical context:** As people began launching emulators, fan games and non-Steam titles through Steam, artwork became part of the user experience.

**Legacy:** It helps retro and non-Steam libraries feel polished without changing the underlying files.

**DAP note:** SteamGridDB is a presentation tool, not a preservation tool.

## Internet Archive

**Category:** Digital library and archival platform.

**Why it matters:** The Internet Archive has preserved an enormous range of digital material: websites, manuals, software, video, audio, books and historical media.

**Historical context:** The web itself is fragile. Pages vanish, companies collapse, services close and documentation disappears. Archival snapshots often become the only surviving record.

**Legacy:** Even when not used directly for games, the Internet Archive is vital for documentation, manuals, store pages, interviews and context.

**DAP note:** Archive links are especially useful for Museum chapters and lost service documentation.

## Flashpoint Archive

**Category:** Web game preservation.

**Why it matters:** Flashpoint Archive preserves web games and animations that depended on browser plugins and web technologies that are no longer widely supported.

**Historical context:** Flash games were a huge part of internet culture. Many were never released in boxed form, never sold through stores and never preserved by their original creators.

**Legacy:** Flashpoint shows how quickly an entire cultural layer can become inaccessible when a runtime disappears.

**DAP note:** Browser-based games deserve preservation too. Not every important game came on a cartridge or disc.

## What This Hall Teaches

The Hall of Fame teaches a simple lesson: preservation is not one job.

It includes:

- emulator development;
- hardware documentation;
- verification databases;
- metadata projects;
- archive platforms;
- engine reimplementations;
- community research;
- frontend polish;
- historical documentation.

A strong retro setup benefits from all of these layers.

## Key Points

- MAME shows that emulation can be documentation.
- Redump and No-Intro show why verification matters.
- TOSEC shows why computer software preservation needs broad cataloguing.
- ScummVM shows that engine reimplementation can be a preservation strategy.
- Dolphin, PCSX2 and RPCS3 show the importance of long-term emulator development.
- ScreenScraper and SteamGridDB improve presentation but should not be confused with verification.
- Internet Archive and Flashpoint remind us that context and web-native games matter.

## What Comes Next

The Hall of Fame celebrates good preservation work. The Hall of Shame does the opposite: it records failures, bad decisions, lost services and avoidable damage so future projects can learn from them.
